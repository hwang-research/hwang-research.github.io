Duty to Dignity at Work (DDW)

Dignity & EthicsEmerging6 items

Employees' volition to refrain from disrespecting others at work: a duty the employee holds toward others, not a perception of how the employee is treated. Introduced as Kantian duty orientation (KDO) in Wang (2024) and named Duty to Dignity at Work (DDW) in Wang, Chen and Scharding (2025).

Theoretical roots

Kant's practical philosophy: the duty not to disrespect others, grounded in the dignity of persons as ends in themselves; and deonance theory (Folger 2001). DDW is distinct from deontic justice (Beugre 2012) and from the duty orientation of Hannah et al. (2014), a utilitarian duty toward a group's members, mission and codes.

How it differs from related scales

Compared with the Workplace Dignity Scale (WDS)

DDW measures a duty the respondent holds toward others: how often the respondent refrains from disrespecting coworkers. The Workplace Dignity Scale measures employees' perceptions of their own dignity at work. In DDW the right to dignity is the object of duty, and the construct concerns the motivation to respect that right and carry out that duty.

Sources: Wang (2024), pp. 9 and 38; Thomas and Lucas (2019), abstract.

Instrument

Response format
Stem: "When interacting with my coworkers, I ..." Seven-point frequency scale, from 1 (almost never) to 7 (always).
Administration
Self-report. Items 3 and 5 are reverse-scored. Single factor; item scores are averaged. An observer form, in which subordinates rate their supervisor, was used in Study 1.4.
  1. Respect others' dignity, even when I think they are clearly wrong.
  2. Treat others with respect, even for people I don't like very much.
  3. Put people down when criticizing their mistakes. (reverse-coded)
  4. Try to understand others' points of view, even if I disagree with them.
  5. Ridicule others when they are at fault. (reverse-coded)
  6. Value people for who they are when others look down on them.

Validity evidence

In Wang (2024), an 18-item pool, drawn from a survey of 37 US business-ethics undergraduates and a faculty seminar review, was reduced to six items on one factor. Across eight samples the six-item model reached CFI .93 to 1.00, SRMR .02 to .04 and RMSEA .00 to .29, with a correlated error between items 3 and 5 (Table 3, p. 65). In Study 1.3, DDW correlated with deontic justice (r = .62), moral identity (r = .54), the duty orientation of Hannah et al. (r = .48), prosocial motivation (r = .29), moral disengagement (r = -.54) and social desirability (r = .31); composite reliability .80, average variance extracted .42 (Table 4, p. 66). Ten-month test-retest r = .65 (ICC2 = .62). In Study 1.4, subordinates' ratings of their supervisor's DDW predicted lower abusive supervision (b = -.52) and higher leader-member exchange (b = .54). Wang, Chen and Scharding (2025) report seven studies with more than 2,500 participants in the United States and China.

Notes

Naming: the dissertation calls the scale the Kantian Duty Orientation (KDO) scale and, in places, the Duty to Dignity scale; Wang, Chen and Scharding (2025) name it the Duty to Dignity at Work (DDW) scale, with the same definition. Items are as printed in the dissertation. Study 2 used a four-item version (alpha .92). In Study 3 the two reverse-scored items loaded .22 and .26. Where the dissertation's text and Table 3 give different sample sizes (Studies 1.3, 2 and 3), each line above cites the source of its value.
